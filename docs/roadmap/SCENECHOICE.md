Yes — the best way to build this is as a **comparison ladder**.

Instead of “8 cool scenes,” I’d give you **8 study types**, where each one trains a different production-design contrast. That way, by the time you finish them, you’ll have studied things like:

* sparse vs crowded
* warm vs cold
* private vs public
* low-status vs high-status
* realism vs stylization
* domestic vs institutional
* harmony vs tension
* architecture-led vs prop-led design

I’d keep **K’s room** as Study 1 or as your “anchor study,” then choose the next studies to deliberately contrast it.

---

# Recommended 8-study ladder

## Study 1 — Cold, sparse, controlled domestic space

**What you learn:** loneliness, low object density, controlled palette, architecture doing emotional work.

You already picked a strong one:

* **Blade Runner 2049** — K’s apartment
* Alternate options:

  * **Gattaca** — Vincent/Jerome apartment
  * **Ex Machina** — Nathan’s house interior zones
  * **Her** — Theodore’s apartment
  * **Never Let Me Go** — restrained private interiors

K’s room is a great anchor because it teaches how a room can feel emotionally specific with very few objects.

---

## Study 2 — Warm, intimate, emotionally lived-in domestic space

**Compare against Study 1:** sparse/cold vs layered/warm.

Choose one:

* **In the Mood for Love** — hallway meals / apartment interior / noodle-shop adjacency
* **Little Women (2019)** — March house interiors
* **The Virgin Suicides** — Lisbon house living spaces
* **Little Fires Everywhere** — family domestic interiors
* **Call Me by Your Name** — family villa living/dining spaces

These are strong because they lean into color, fabric, routine, closeness, and memory rather than isolation. AD describes *In the Mood for Love* as rich in color, pattern, mood lighting, and texture; it also covered *Little Women* and *Little Fires Everywhere* as production-design-heavy recreations of lived-in worlds. ([Architectural Digest][1])

**Good scenes to choose from:**

* kitchen or dining area
* hallway conversation area
* bedroom corner
* family sitting room
* doorway/compressed transition space

---

## Study 3 — Class contrast through housing

**What you learn:** how space shows wealth, aspiration, precarity, and social structure.

Choose one:

* **Parasite** — Park house vs Kim semi-basement
* **Little Fires Everywhere** — Richardson home vs Warren apartment
* **Snowpiercer** — front cars vs tail section
* **Roma** — household/service space relationships
* **Shoplifters** — cramped shared domestic space

*Parasite* is almost perfect for this category because the production design is explicitly built on contrast between the Kims’ semi-basement and the Parks’ sleek modern house. ([Architectural Digest][2])

**Good scenes to choose from:**

* living room of a rich household
* cramped meal scene in a lower-income home
* bathroom / utility zone
* stair transition between social worlds
* threshold shot looking from interior to exterior

---

## Study 4 — Period realism without fantasy overload

**What you learn:** how to study historical specificity without needing “grand spectacle.”

Choose one:

* **Little Women (2019)** — everyday March family rooms
* **Mad Men** — Don Draper apartment / office lounge / diner
* **The King** — court and chamber interiors
* **Carol** — department store / apartment / restaurant interiors
* **Brooklyn** — boarding house / family home / workplace interiors

These are useful because they are period-specific, but the design is still rooted in daily life. AD’s *Mad Men* feature emphasizes the show’s researched 1960s authenticity and evolving interiors, and its *Little Women* and *The King* coverage highlights historically grounded design choices. ([Architectural Digest][3])

**Good scenes to choose from:**

* dining room
* office / desk zone
* bedroom vanity area
* hallway / staircase
* public interior like diner or shop

---

## Study 5 — Opulence, power, and stylized status

**Compare against Study 4:** realistic period life vs theatrical power spaces.

Choose one:

* **The Favourite** — Queen Anne interiors
* **The Great** — palace rooms
* **Bridgerton** — Bridgerton house vs Featherington house
* **Rebecca (2020)** — Manderley interiors
* **Marie Antoinette** — bedroom/salon/banquet spaces

These are good because power is expressed through scale, ornament, symmetry, and controlled excess. AD describes *The Favourite* as lush but tightly focused, and its pieces on *The Great*, *Bridgerton*, and *Rebecca* all discuss how design encodes status and character through rooms. ([Architectural Digest][1])

**Good scenes to choose from:**

* throne or audience room
* bedchamber
* banquet or dining hall
* dressing room
* long corridor with ceremonial feeling

---

## Study 6 — Architecture as character

**What you learn:** when the building itself carries meaning, not just the decor.

Choose one:

* **Parasite** — Park house
* **Where’d You Go, Bernadette** — Bernadette’s home
* **Rebecca** — Manderley
* **Blade Runner 2049** — apartment building / hallway / room as system
* **The Grand Budapest Hotel** — lobby or suite spaces

This category is about reading a set almost like a person. AD’s *Parasite* and *Where’d You Go, Bernadette* coverage are especially strong on this point: both pieces discuss how the architecture was deliberately designed to express worldview and character. ([Architectural Digest][2])

**Good scenes to choose from:**

* exterior-to-interior arrival
* staircase sequence
* large room with strong geometry
* threshold shot
* a room where the architecture dominates the human figure

---

## Study 7 — Institutional or procedural spaces with emotional subtext

**What you learn:** how “functional” spaces still tell story.

Choose one:

* **The Shape of Water** — lab / Elisa’s apartment contrast
* **Snowpiercer** — train compartments
* **Mad Men** — office bullpen / conference rooms
* **Blade Runner 2049** — police / bureaucratic interiors
* **Arrival** — military/scientific staging areas

This is a valuable category because production design is often strongest when it makes a work space or system space feel emotionally loaded. AD’s *Shape of Water*, *Snowpiercer*, and *Mad Men* pieces all point to highly constructed worlds where function and mood are inseparable. ([Architectural Digest][4])

**Good scenes to choose from:**

* lab room
* office bullpen
* control room
* train/dining/service compartment
* locker or prep area

---

## Study 8 — Confinement, thresholds, and transition spaces

**What you learn:** hallways, stairs, doors, and in-between spaces as design tools.

Choose one:

* **In the Mood for Love** — corridor and stair interactions
* **Parasite** — stairways, basement thresholds, entries
* **Blade Runner 2049** — apartment hallway and door zone
* **Rebecca** — corridors and looming passage spaces
* **The Shining** — hotel hallway geometry

These scenes are excellent because they train you to see that production design is not only about “the main room.” Thresholds often carry class tension, secrecy, intimacy, surveillance, or dread. *Parasite*, *Rebecca*, and *In the Mood for Love* are especially good for this kind of study. ([Architectural Digest][2])

**Good scenes to choose from:**

* stair landing
* front door entry
* apartment corridor
* narrow passage
* transition from public to private room

# A practical rule for choosing the exact scene

For each study, choose a scene that has at least 3 of these:

* one strong room or spatial unit
* visible props and surfaces
* clear lighting logic
* emotional meaning tied to the set
* enough screenshots available
* not too much action blur or chaos

---

# Keep it light

For each of these 8 studies, you do **not** need to do everything.

Early on, I’d rotate emphasis:

* one study focuses more on **space**
* one on **palette**
* one on **props**
* one on **script-to-set**
* one on **thresholds/circulation**

That keeps the workload sustainable.
